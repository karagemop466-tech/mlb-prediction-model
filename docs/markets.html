One simulation, many markets. Instead of training a separate model per question, the system simulates each game inning by inning 12,000 times and reads every market off the same joint distribution. That makes the answers coherent by construction: the margin probabilities sum to the win probability, and no conjunction can exceed its marginals.

Skill compares the Brier score against always predicting the base rate. Positive means the model discriminates between games; near zero means it is well-calibrated but the outcome is essentially a league constant.

The honest read: the winner market and the conjunctions carry real skill. Structural markets like one-run games and extra innings are calibrated (bias under 0.01) but carry almost no per-game skill — whether a specific game ends by one run is close to irreducible noise. Reporting that rather than dressing up a near-zero number as an edge.

First-five-innings markets (2026-08-01)

The simulator already generates runs inning by inning, so first-five-innings markets came almost free. They are also a genuine out-of-sample validation of the generative model: F5 was never a fitting target, yet the simulator reproduces it closely — total 5.11 vs 5.11 actual, tie rate 15.2% vs 14.8%, home-lead rate 44.5% vs 45.5%.

F5 home lead now carries the second-highest skill of any market (+0.0150), behind only the full-game winner and ahead of totals. The starter is still pitching, so team-quality signal is less diluted by bullpen variance.

Side-specific run model (2026-07-31 upgrade)

The first version derived both teams' scoring rates from the win probability plus a single expected total. That total came from a hand-coded heuristic with a spread of only sd 0.373 across games and 0.116 correlation with reality — nearly a constant, which is why the totals market showed almost no skill.

It is now a learned model predicting home and away runs separately, because home scoring depends on home offense and away pitching. Correlation with the realized total rose to 0.134 (1.67x), and the model can represent matchups the old inversion could not: a strong offense facing a strong pitcher has a high win probability and a low total. Two matchups with win probabilities of 0.600 and 0.596 now produce expected totals of 10.29 and 7.26.

The walk-off asymmetry

Home teams win by exactly one run 16.69% of the time but lose by exactly one run only 11.08% of the time — 51% more often. This is not team strength; it is the rule that the game stops the moment the home team takes the lead in the ninth. No model of final scores can produce that from strength parameters. The simulator reproduces it (ratio 1.59 vs actual 1.51) because it applies the stopping rule during play rather than fitting the outcome.

Pricing and coherence

Probabilities convert to fair American and decimal odds, with an optional vig for comparison against posted numbers. The coherence auditor checks quote sets against Fréchet bounds and decomposition identities — it catches, for example, a book quoting P(win)=0.60, P(over)=0.55 and P(win AND over)=0.62, which is arithmetically impossible. No ROI is claimed and no edge over any real market is asserted.
